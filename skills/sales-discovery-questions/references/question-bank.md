## Frame (1 line)

- "Fair if we spend 25 minutes on where things break down for you, then decide together whether a deeper look makes sense?" (applies Sandler's upfront-contract concept: agree purpose, time, and possible outcomes upfront.)

## Situation (cap at 1-2)

Research replaces situation questions - asking facts the company publishes burns credibility. Keep only what research cannot answer.

- "Walk me through how your team handles [process] today." (SPIN Situation wording as restated by a vendor publisher.)
- "Who touches that process day to day, and what tools does it live in?"

## Pain

- "What's the biggest hurdle you're facing right now with [goal]?" (SPIN Problem restatement.)
- "What's the hardest part of [process] as it works today?"
- "When did it last go wrong? Walk me through what happened." (past-specific anchor.)
- Outbound variant - offer a point of view first, then test it: share the pattern seen at similar companies in one sentence, then "how close is that to your world?" (outbound buyers need problem awareness built, not assumed.)

Follow-up ladder (Sandler Pain Funnel ordering, published by a vendor; the ordering vague → specific → duration → attempts → cost → emotion is the transferable part):

1. "Tell me more about that."
2. "Can you be more specific? Give me an example."
3. "How long has that been a problem?"

## Root cause

- "Why do you think that keeps happening?"
- "What have you tried to do about it?" then "Did that work?" (Sandler funnel rungs.)
- "What would you say is underneath that - people, process, or the tooling?" (closed follow-up, only after the open version.)
- Listen-for: a cause stated as a symptom ("we're just too busy") - one more rung down before accepting it.

## Impact

Quantify on at least two of the three GAP Selling dimensions. Which dimension gets the next question, when the call cannot fund all three:

- efficiency: `business > personal > technical`
- value: `business > technical > personal`
- effort: `technical > business == personal`

- Business and personal tie on effort: each costs one question and one follow-up asked inside a stage the buyer is already in, the same minute either way, and the same small draw on their patience.
- Business wins efficiency: its answer is the figure the close, the recap and every downstream deal skill run on.
- Technical costs more than both on effort: it pulls the buyer into explaining a system, and most of what it returns the root-cause stage already surfaced.

Starved: technical impact, genuinely high value (it is the only dimension an architect or a security reviewer can answer with a real number) but it loses every round on effort with anyone else.

- Promote it to first when the persona _is_ the technical evaluator, or when the pain is an outage, a reliability problem, or a data loss.
- Delete it, rather than compressing it, on a short call: two funded dimensions beat three rushed ones, and the gate wants two.

This ordering is a default, not a law; re-rank it against the persona in front of you before drafting. B2C runs `personal > business > technical` - household time, money and stress _is_ the business case there.

- Technical: "When that fails, what does it do to everything downstream?"
- Business: "What do you think that costs you per month - time, money, or deals?" (Sandler cost rung, merged with figure-first framing.)
- Business: "If [necessary task] doesn't happen, what's the impact on [team]'s numbers?" (SPIN Implication restatement.)
- Personal: "What does that mean for you and your team, week to week?"
- Personal / emotional weight: "How do you feel about what this has already cost you?" (Sandler funnel rung.)
- Spread: "Why do you want it, and who else does it impact?" (Samantha McKenna, via a vendor roundup.)
- Framing note, a vendor claim: mentioning the seller's ROI math during discovery correlates with worse next-step rates; cost-of-inaction framing - their number, their loss - performs better.

## Urgency / compelling event

MEDDPICC's Three Whys: "Why anything? Why you? Why now?" - discovery owns "why anything" and "why now"; "why you" is positioning, later.

- "What happens if this isn't solved in the next six months?" (cost-of-inaction form.)
- "What's changed recently that makes this worth looking at now?" (hunts the trigger event.)
- "Where does fixing this sit on this quarter's priority list?"
- "Is there a date this becomes a real problem - renewal, launch, audit, season?"
- Caveat: if nothing is found, record "no compelling event" honestly. Manufactured urgency deepens buyer indecision (Dixon & McKenna's published buyer-indecision research, their own claim).

## Decision context (complex B2B only - questions, never scores)

Gather these to feed the scorecard skill; never output a qualification verdict here.

- "If you decided this was worth solving, what would the path to a yes look like?" (decision process.)
- "Who else would want a say - and who could veto it?"
- "What would you need to see to know it worked?" (metrics.)
- "Have you evaluated anything for this before? What happened?" (competition incl. do-nothing.)
- Regulated buyers: add "what does security / compliance / procurement review look like for a purchase like this?"

## Close

- Summarize in their words: "So if I've got it - [pain], because [root cause], costing you [their figure]. What did I miss?"
- "Given that, would [specific next step] on [date] make sense?" (dated, specific, tied to the impact just quantified.)

## B2C high-ticket variants

- Pain: "What finally made you book this conversation?" (the trigger event, asked first.)
- Root cause / prior attempts: "What's kept you from doing this until now?"
- Personal impact leads: "What would getting this handled change for you day to day?"
- Household-business impact: "What is living with it costing you - time, money, stress?"
- Couples: ask pain and impact to each person separately - "what do you each most want out of this?" Two decision makers, two spines.
- Urgency: "Why now, after [duration they gave]?"
- Regulated categories (financial advice, health): flag phrasing constraints to the user rather than guessing the rules.

## Do-not-ask list

Put these on every call sheet as written warnings:

- "What's the timeline for this project?" and "Is there budget allocated for this?" as openers - negative examples published by GAP Selling practitioners: they harvest logistics before establishing that a problem exists. Both become legitimate late, after impact.
- Hypothetical purchase questions - "would you use / buy / pay for X?" Answers are politeness, not data; ask about past behavior instead.
- Leading questions - "that must be costing you a fortune, right?" The prospect's number persuades; the rep's number gets argued with.
- Compound questions - "what's broken and what have you tried?" Ask one, ladder to the other.
- Filler check-ins - "does that make sense?" - and any question asked to fill silence rather than to learn.
